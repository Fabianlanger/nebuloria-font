Why Nebuloria

Nebuloria began as a signature: a name for a website that needed one human, crafted moment between calm, geometric text.

The brief was a feeling, not a font. Victorian conservatories, where cast iron carries glass and plants climb the columns. And the see-through electronics of the late nineties, where you could look into a telephone and see its wires. And the futurism of that same moment: organic, aerodynamic shapes from aerospace, and the straight lines of microchips. Eras that all made structure visible, and that together describe an optimistic, green and elegant future: refined solarpunk.

The typeface was not drawn by hand. It was directed: by learning the language of type design, turning a mood into precise rules, and letting AI models draw and redraw until the rules held. Every principle in this book started as a sentence.

Nebuloria is a display face. It is made for large sizes: wordmarks, headlines, pull quotes. It comes in two styles: Regular, calm and readable, and Hekwerk (Dutch for "iron fence"), which opens a window between every pair of upright stems.

Two worlds, one letter

Iron and glass

The Crystal Palace, London, 1851. Lithograph by J. McNeven. Public domain.
Palm House, Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew. Photo: Daniel Case, CC BY-SA 3.0.

The Victorians built cathedrals for plants: heavy iron ribs carrying hair-thin glazing bars, and between them glass.

From this world Nebuloria takes its structure: heavy strokes next to hairlines, scrolls that grow from the line, the octagon and the 45° cut. Not its shine: no gold, no chrome, no metallic effects.

Two worlds, one letter

See-through

Transparent telephone with visible wiring. Photo: Ellinor Algin / Swedish National Museum of Science and Technology, CC BY 4.0.
iMac G3, translucent Bondi blue, 1998. Photo: katz2110 (Flickr), CC BY 2.0.

Around the year 2000, electronics turned transparent. The casing became a window, and the circuit inside became decoration.

A font cannot be transparent: it is one colour. So translucency had to become form: hairlines, open glass panes, wires that run behind a stroke and show through it.

Fiction got there first: the salon of the Nautilus in Jules Verne's Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Seas (1870), a Victorian future of iron, glass and engineering. Engraving by A. de Neuville and É. Riou. Public domain.

Two worlds, one letter

Future forms

Blended-wing-body aircraft concept. NASA / The Boeing Company, public domain.

Y2K futurism was organic and aerodynamic: one surface that swells, carries its weight and tapers out to a sharp edge.

That is the weight profile of every Nebuloria stroke: full and heavy, then running out into a needle point, like a wing into its tip.

Its other half is the microchip: straight lines, right angles and 45° turns, packed with precision. From it come the chamfered corners, the windows and the glare lines.

Die of an integrated circuit: straight lines and 45° routing. Photo: Cole L, CC BY-SA 2.0.
Two worlds
A name for the blend

Solarpunk

Put the three together: the Victorian glasshouse, transparent technology and organic engineering. What you get is a future where technology and nature grow into each other.

That future has a name: solarpunk. Nebuloria speaks its elegant dialect: not makeshift or rustic, but refined, crafted and precise. Quiet luxury in glass, iron and green. Solar glass in an orangery roof, vines on slender frames, cables and roots running side by side.

It is a natural fit for high-end solarpunk worlds: botanical restaurants and hotels, wayfinding for gardens and museums, fine publishing, perfume and tea, games and film titles that imagine a hopeful, beautifully made future.

Six principles · 1

Heavy and hairline

Broad-edged and pointed nibs. Photo: R. Henrik Nilsson, CC BY 4.0.

Every main stroke behaves as if drawn with a broad nib held at 45°: strokes from top-left to bottom-right are heavy, strokes from top-right to bottom-left are hair-thin. It is the same contrast as iron ribs against glazing bars.

Six principles · 2

The stroke pair

Wrought-iron gate. Photo: MauraWen, CC BY 4.0.
FH

In a wrought-iron gate, thin scrolls grow alongside the heavy bars. In Nebuloria every capital is a stroke pair: a heavy stroke and a thin, wavy tendril that starts at the same chamfered corner and runs beside it, ending in a small teardrop.

The two have mirrored weight: the main stroke is heavy and tapers to a needle point; the tendril is a hairline that swells slightly at its end.

Principles
Six principles · 3

Forty-five degrees

Octagonal cast-iron bandstand. Photo: Rodhullandemu, CC BY-SA 4.0.
Cbmn

Where a classic letter has a round corner, Nebuloria has a straight cut at 45°.

Bowls and arches are built like the Victorian octagon: straight sides joined by chamfers. The cut is where the stroke turns thin. Diagonals and curls only travel one way, from north-east to south-west, the direction of the pen.

This is also where the Y2K world comes in: the chamfer is the bevelled edge of moulded plastic and the 45° route of a circuit trace.

Six principles · 4

A leaf in the counter

Backlit leaf. Photo: Jon Sullivan, public domain.

A conservatory is a house for plants, so the plants are inside the letters. In every closed bowl (a, b, d, g, o, p, q) one small leaf grows from the inside of the stroke into the counter.

Botanical, not floral: the leaf grows from the stroke. Nothing is stuck on top, and there are no flowers.

Six principles · 5

The window

Dome of the Kibble Palace, Glasgow. Photo: Lib79, CC BY-SA 4.0.
Printed circuit board with 45° traces. Photo: Mister rf, CC BY-SA 4.0.
an

When a letter ending in an upright stem meets a letter starting with one (as in an, il, hu), the two stems are joined by thin bridges and enclose a new counter: a window.

Inside, three short 45° lines. They read twice: as the glare on a pane of glass, and as circuit traces seen through clear plastic. One shape, both worlds.

Six principles · 6

The wire: cable and vine

Liana in a rainforest. Photo: Dick Culbert, CC BY 2.0.
Bond wires connecting a chip. Photo: Zephyris, CC BY-SA 3.0.
Ribbon cable. Photo: Retired electrician, CC0.
Langer

Between some letters a thin wire grows: from the top-right of one letter, hanging in a soft round curve, into the top-left of the next. It is a vine and a cable at once: the liana on the conservatory column and the wire inside the transparent phone. Above all, it connects: letters into words, and the two worlds into one.

Principles
And what Nebuloria is not

Leave out

  • Metallic effects, chrome, gloss, gold. The iron is structure, not shine.
  • Flowers or leaves stuck on top of letters. Everything grows from the stroke.
  • Fairy-tale "vine lettering". Lianas are rare and precise.
  • Art deco and blackletter. The 45° cut is a detail, not a costume.
  • Uniform, monolinear strokes. Contrast is the backbone.
  • Round, soft blobs. Every end is a needle point, a teardrop or a crisp cut.
  • Small sizes. Nebuloria starts at 40 px; its details need 72 px and up.

Using the font

Set it free

Nebuloria is free for everyone, for personal and commercial work. Here is how to get it and use it, no technical knowledge needed.

1 · Download

Go to github.com/Fabianlanger/nebuloria-font and download the newest Nebuloria zip file (at the time of writing Nebuloria-v1.6.zip; newer versions add more characters). Unzip the file (double-click it).

2 · Install

Windows: open the folder fonts/otf, right-click both files and choose Install.
Mac: double-click each file and click Install Font.

Both styles now appear in your font menu: Nebuloria and Nebuloria Hekwerk.

3 · Use

Pick Nebuloria in Word, PowerPoint, Keynote, Pages, Figma, Photoshop or Illustrator. Use it big: titles, names, posters, covers. Set the rest of your text in a calm, simple font.

Windows and wires

The glass windows and hanging wires are a hidden extra called discretionary ligatures. Switch them on where your app allows it:

WordFont › Advanced › Ligatures: All
FigmaType settings › Details › Discretionary ligatures

For the iron fence, choose the style Nebuloria Hekwerk. Apps without these options show the clean letters, which still look great.

Figures are lining: as tall as the capitals, with the same flags, chamfers and leaves. Every dot is the chamfered diamond of the i. Accented letters are not redrawn: each is the font's own letter with one drawn accent on top, so an é is always exactly the e.

How it was made · 1

From words to letters

Nebuloria was designed by learning the vocabulary of type design and using it to direct AI. Each round followed one loop: learn a term → write a rule → render with an image model → judge → refine the rule.

v0.1 · The first F: "stroke pairs", "leaf terminals".
v0.1 · Too many leaves; the model drifts to fairy-tale lettering.
v0.2 · "Chamfers" without a dose: everything becomes angular.
v0.2 · Langer: the sharpness that became the standard.
v0.3 · The window ligature, defined for letter classes.
v0.4 · The target image.

The biggest lesson: words have a dose. "Chamfered corners" without a limit means "everywhere"; "at most one small leaf" means "none". A precise brief says what, how much and where.

Process
How it was made · 2

From images to a font

1 · Glyph sheets: one row of letters per image.
2 · Letters that felt too basic were redrawn with stricter rules.
3 · For wires, the real letters were rendered in grey…
4 · …and only the black wire was drawn on top and extracted.

Every letter was cut from its sheet, traced into vector outlines, set on a shared baseline and x-height, and built into an OpenType font with Python (fontTools). Spacing is measured from each letter's heavy body, so tendrils and tails may reach into their neighbours. Windows and wires are OpenType rules, so they appear by themselves while typing.
